The robot

Meet The Phoenix.

Named for the bird on our crest. Nothing on this robot was designed in isolation — each mechanism was prototyped on the bot, tested in matches, then kept, changed, or cut.

⚙

Mecanum drivetrain

Four mecanum wheels, each driven by a 312 RPM motor through a miter gear. Omnidirectional motion and fast rotation give us an edge over a slower tank drive.

Omnidirectional312 RPMMiter gears
🔄

Boot-wheel intake

Seven 96 mm boot wheels on a single Rex shaft, chain-driven by a 312 RPM motor. The wheels interlock with the holes in the artifacts for a far more reliable grab than surface contact alone.

7 boot wheelsGecko-wheel feedCompliance
🎯

Flywheel launcher

A 3D-printed fixed hood centers each artifact and holds a consistent 45° compression. Two hogback wheels plus steel flywheels add rotational inertia for higher throughput and shorter cycle times.

6000 RPMFixed hoodServo gate
🕹

Driver controls

Two Logitech F310 controllers split motion from scoring. Translation and rotation live on controller one, inspired by modern console games; flywheel, lever, and feeder live on controller two.

Autonomous positioning

Odometry pods and a Limelight camera let the robot localize and align without human input — the biggest upgrade we made between our qualifier and the State Championship.

Engineering process

The Integrated Design Pipeline

Most teams brainstorm, CAD, analyze, then build. We found we learn faster by testing ideas on the actual robot — so each step feeds directly into the next instead of standing alone.

01BrainstormIdentify the problem and sketch options.
02Rapid prototypeBuild it on the bot, not just on screen.
03Test in contextMeasure it against real match conditions.
04DecideKeep, modify, or remove the mechanism.
05IterateFold the result into the current design.

Our odometry pods and Limelight arrived six days before the State Championship. Because the pipeline is built for fast physical iteration, they went from unboxing to a working autonomous routine in under a week.
